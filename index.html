About

About

I’m in the final year of a PhD in aerospace engineering at Texas A&M University, working with Elaine Oran on how detonations quench.

A detonation is a combustion wave that runs ahead of itself — the shock compresses the gas hard enough to ignite it, and the released heat drives the shock. Send one into a channel narrow enough and it usually dies. But not always, and not always for the reasons you would guess. I simulate that whole sequence: flame acceleration, the transition from deflagration to detonation, propagation, and then the quenching or reinitiation at the end.

The applied version of the question is how to build a detonation arrestor — the device that stops a flame front from travelling down a gas pipeline. That matters more now that hydrogen is being blended into infrastructure originally designed for methane, since the two behave quite differently once something ignites.

The work is reactive-flow computation on HPC clusters, mostly using AMReX. A good share of it is resolution: detonation cell structure only appears once the grid is fine enough, so before trusting a result I usually have to establish what resolution that particular result actually needed.

Before Texas A&M I did a master’s in propulsion engineering in Trivandrum, on supersonic intakes, and a short stint at IIT Madras on thermal resistance at solid–fluid interfaces using molecular dynamics. Outside of research I draw, play soccer, collect Japanese manga, and am slowly working through Japanese and Chinese.

Interests

What I’m working on

  • Detonation quenching and reinitiation

    What happens when a detonation enters a channel narrow enough to kill it — wall heat loss, the three-dimensional structure of the front, and the cases where the wave comes back instead of dying.

  • Transition to detonation in fuel blends

    Run-up distances and detonation cell sizes in hydrogen–methane mixtures, which is the practical question behind blending hydrogen into existing natural gas pipelines.

  • Shock transmission from open tubes

    What the shock does once it leaves the end of a detonation tube, and how it interacts with the exhaust gases trailing behind it.
